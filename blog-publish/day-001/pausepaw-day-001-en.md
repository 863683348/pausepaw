# Average Daily Screen Time in 2026: What the Data Says

If you picked up your phone right now, would you want to know how many hours you've already spent on it today? If your gut says "probably too many", you're far from alone.

In 2026, the average person now spends more of their waking hours looking at a screen than doing almost anything else. Here is what the latest data actually says — and what it means for you.

## The headline number

Across the major 2026 studies, **adults spend 6 hours and 40 minutes per day looking at screens**, up from 6 hours 23 minutes in 2025. Phones account for roughly half of that. Teens, as you would expect, run higher — averaging **8 hours and 30 minutes per day** of recreational screen time, of which 4 hours 20 minutes is on a phone.

For comparison: people in the 1980s averaged about 3 hours a day. We have roughly doubled screen exposure in 40 years.

## Where the time actually goes

- **Social media** — 2h 17m
- **Video streaming** (Netflix, YouTube, TikTok, Twitch) — 1h 58m
- **Messaging and email** — 49m
- **Web browsing (general)** — 38m
- **Gaming** — 35m
- **Other** — 23m

A few patterns stand out. Short-form video is now the single biggest category, having overtaken long-form streaming in 2024. And once you combine "social media plus video streaming", you are looking at **4 hours and 15 minutes per day** — more than half of all screen time.

## The country breakdown

Average daily screen time varies wildly by region:

| Country           | Avg daily screen time |
| ----------------- | --------------------- |
| South Africa      | 9h 24m                |
| Brazil            | 9h 03m                |
| Philippines       | 8h 52m                |
| United States     | 7h 06m                |
| United Kingdom    | 6h 43m                |
| Japan             | 4h 27m                |
| Germany           | 4h 14m                |

(These numbers come from aggregated 2026 reports from DataReportal, Statista, and Rescuetime.)

If you live in a high-screen-time country and have tried to "cut back", you have probably noticed: **the social pressure to be constantly available is real, and willpower is not enough**.

## The "phone-only" subset

Phones deserve their own number, because they are the most portable and the hardest to put down:

- Adults pick up their phone **roughly 144 times per day** on average.
- The phone is checked within **10 minutes of waking** by 79 percent of adults.
- **65 percent of adults check their phone within 5 minutes of receiving a notification.**

This is the screen-time category where small reductions have the biggest effect on focus, sleep, and stress. Cutting phone time by 1 hour per day is more impactful than cutting TV by 1 hour, because phones are the most interrupt-driven surface you own.

## What the numbers do not tell you

Averages hide two important things:

1. **Distribution is skewed.** The top 20 percent of screen-time users account for roughly half of all hours. Many people use their phone a healthy amount; a small group uses it a lot.
2. **Recreational vs. work screen time is collapsing into one number.** If you work a desk job, your total screen time will be high, but only some of it is voluntary.

So when you see "7 hours a day" — that is not a verdict on your habits. It is a starting point for asking a more useful question: **how much of this is what I actually want?**

## What to do with this number

If you are curious where you stand:

1. Open your phone's built-in screen time tool (iOS: Settings → Screen Time; Android: Settings → Digital Wellbeing).
2. Look at last week's daily average, broken down by app category.
3. Ask: *which category would I most like to shrink?*

Most people who do this pick one of three categories: social media, short-form video, or messaging. **Pick one category, not all three.** Pick a daily cap (e.g. 60 minutes). Then let a tool enforce it for you, because tracking alone has a poor track record.
